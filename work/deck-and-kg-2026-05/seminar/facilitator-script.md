# Facilitator Script — *Thinking Spatially About the Past*
### Companion to the NotebookLM slide deck "The Layered Archive"

**Audience:** junior undergraduates, new to spatial history.
**Total:** ~70 min + Q&A. **Tone:** concrete over abstract — lead with examples, name theory lightly.
**You'll need:** the slide deck open, one printed historical map of Saigon per pair (or a live VMA screen), the one-page handout distributed at the start.

> **Repeat this sentence at the open and the close:** *"History usually asks* when *something happened. Spatial history also asks* where *— and treats 'where' as evidence."*

---

## 0 · Open (2 min)
- Distribute the handout. "Don't read ahead — we'll walk it together."
- Say the one-sentence pitch. Promise: "By the end you'll know what spatial history is, see real projects doing it, and meet one we're building for Saigon."

## ACT 1 — What is spatial history? (18 min)

**Hook (4 min).** Put up two maps of the same ground: a pre-modern Vietnamese map (*non nước* — "mountain and water," a travel itinerary, no coordinate grid) and a French colonial cadastral plan of Saigon.
- Ask the room: *"Which one is correct?"* Let them argue.
- Land it: **Neither is just 'correct.' Each is an argument about what space is and who controls it.** That's the whole field in one comparison.

**The core shift (5 min).** Traditional history = space is the *stage*. Spatial history = space is *produced*, it *changes*, and it *shapes what people can do*.
- Everyday anchor: a railway line, a segregated quarter, a colonial grid — not just locations, but *decisions with consequences*.

**The theory, lightly (5 min).** Don't lecture — name and move on:
- Lefebvre: official/planned space ≠ lived space.
- Massey: space is "always under construction."
- Soja: the "spatial turn." Carter: coined the term (1987).
- **Harley (dwell here):** *maps are arguments, not mirrors.* Every map includes, erases, and serves someone. Reading that is historical analysis.

**How it's done (4 min).** Three method families — keep it to a sentence each: HGIS (georeference → digitize → link → overlay), deep mapping (memories, photos, oral history), geographical text analysis (place-names from text).
- *Check for understanding:* "What's the difference between a map as a mirror and a map as an argument?" Take one answer.

## ACT 1.5 — What questions does it let you ask? (10 min)
*This is the "why care" payoff — give it energy.*
- Walk the question-type table on the handout. For each, tell the story in one breath:
  - **Clustering** — Snow's cholera map: the *pattern* was the proof.
  - **Hidden causes** — Cunfer's Dust Bowl: the map overturned "greedy farmers."
  - **Who was erased** — DeBats: geocoding records put excluded voters back on the map.
  - **Constraint** — Knowles at Gettysburg: what could the general actually *see*?
  - **Lived vs. official** — Lutz: "Chinatown" was porous, not the block elites described.
- **Drive it home:** "Every one of these is a claim the written archive *hid*. The map surfaced it. That's the point of the method."

## ACT 2 — The field is alive (10 min)
- Quick tour, one line each — students should leave knowing this is a real discipline:
  - **Stanford Spatial History Project** — the flagship lab.
  - **ORBIS** — the Roman world measured in *travel time*, not distance (relational space made visible).
  - **CHGIS** — China's changing geography over centuries; the long-term infrastructure model.
  - **SODUCO** — mapping Paris automatically from old city directories (our closest cousin).
  - **Locating London's Past**, **Allmaps** (the open georeferencing tool we build on).
- Transition: *"Each turned an archive into something you can question. So — what's the equivalent for Saigon?"*

## ACT 3 — VMA: spatial history in practice (20 min)

**A. VMA instantiates the theory (6 min).** Don't re-teach — *map theory → feature*:
- Lefebvre → VMA holds colonial planners' maps *and* community memory as comparable layers.
- Harley → VMA re-reads French cadastral maps as instruments of property/power (bring in **de Rugy** on imperial borders, **Clayton** on "geographical warfare" and the colonial "rule of experts").
- Harvey/ORBIS → the **knowledge graph** = relational space (owners, families, events).
- Massey → places have start/end dates; the city is modeled as always under construction.

**B. What VMA can do (7 min).** Frame each as a *research act*, ideally with a live screen:
1. Georeference historical Saigon maps → *see what the colonial plan did to the ground.*
2. Vectorize building footprints → *turn a picture into queryable data.*
3. Build the knowledge graph → *reconstruct the lived city the grid erased.*
4. Crowdsource with volunteers → *a community archive — and a counter-mapping experiment.*
5. Layer across time → *watch Saigon transform across planning regimes.*
- **Honest tension to raise:** "Are we decolonizing the colonial archive, or re-inscribing its grid by using the colonizers' coordinate system?" — leave it open. Good seminar fuel.

**C. The questions VMA answers (7 min).** Tie back to Act 1.5's types, now about Saigon:
- *Hidden causes:* **How did successive planning regimes encode spatial inequality into the built form — and does that inheritance still shape the city today?** (our binding question)
- *Who was erased:* which Vietnamese residents/uses did the cadastre leave off?
- *Lived vs. official:* did the planned colonial city match the one people inhabited?

## Close + activity (8 min)
- Hand each pair a Saigon map (or point at the VMA screen) and the three questions from the handout:
  1. What does this map want you to believe — and whom does it leave off?
  2. What would you turn into data — and what would that choice hide?
  3. What could it answer that a text never could?
- Take 2–3 answers aloud. Then repeat the one-sentence pitch to close.
- *(If recruiting volunteers: end on "VMA is built by people like you — come trace a map or add a family history.")*

## Q&A — likely questions + crisp answers
- **"Isn't this just making maps?"** No — maps are the *output*; the research is the questions the spatial data lets you ask and answer.
- **"Is GIS objective?"** No. The boundaries and categories you choose shape the result (the MAUP problem). A coordinate is still an interpretation.
- **"Why colonial maps if they're biased?"** Because reading the bias *is* the analysis — and crowdsourcing lets us add back what they erased.
- **"Do I need to code?"** No. Reading a map critically and asking a spatial question is the skill. Tools like Allmaps and VMA lower the technical barrier.
